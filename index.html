Overview

This project builds a path tracer from camera rays and primitive intersections through global illumination. The BVH makes intersection tests faster, importance sampling reduces wasted lighting samples, and adaptive sampling spends less time on pixels that have converged.

Part 1: Ray Generation and Scene Intersection

Each pixel uses ns_aa random offsets from the uniform square sampler. Dividing the resulting positions by image width and height gives normalized coordinates with the origin at the bottom left. The pixel stores the average RGB radiance and the number of samples taken.

The pinhole camera looks along its local negative z axis. Normalized image coordinates \((x,y)\) map to the sensor point

\[ \mathbf{s}=\left((2x-1)\tan\frac{h\pi}{360},\ (2y-1)\tan\frac{v\pi}{360},\ -1\right), \]

Here, \(h\) and \(v\) are the horizontal and vertical fields of view in degrees. The camera matrix rotates this direction into world space, where it is normalized. The ray starts at the camera position and uses the near and far clipping distances. This follows the ray tracing lecture.

Part 1 initially checks every primitive. Each hit shortens max_t so that only the closest intersection remains. The figures map its normal to RGB with \(\mathbf{c}=(\mathbf{n}+\mathbf{1})/2\), while missed rays return black. These colors show surface orientation. The BVH added in Part 2 preserves this shading.

Möller–Trumbore solves for the ray distance and barycentric coordinates. With triangle edges \(\mathbf{e}_1=\mathbf{p}_2-\mathbf{p}_1\) and \(\mathbf{e}_2=\mathbf{p}_3-\mathbf{p}_1\), ray origin \(\mathbf{o}\), and direction \(\mathbf{d}\),

\[ \mathbf{o}+t\mathbf{d}=\mathbf{p}_1+u\mathbf{e}_1+v\mathbf{e}_2. \]

Define \(\mathbf{p}=\mathbf{d}\times\mathbf{e}_2\), \(\Delta=\mathbf{e}_1\cdot\mathbf{p}\), \(\mathbf{s}=\mathbf{o}-\mathbf{p}_1\), and \(\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{s}\times\mathbf{e}_1\). Then

\[ u=\frac{\mathbf{s}\cdot\mathbf{p}}{\Delta},\qquad v=\frac{\mathbf{d}\cdot\mathbf{q}}{\Delta},\qquad t=\frac{\mathbf{e}_2\cdot\mathbf{q}}{\Delta}. \]

A zero determinant rejects parallel rays and degenerate triangles. A hit requires \(u\geq0\), \(v\geq0\), \(u+v\leq1\), and \(t\) within the ray interval. Either determinant sign is accepted, allowing hits from both sides.

An accepted hit updates max_t and stores the distance, primitive, material, and normalized interpolated normal \((1-u-v)\mathbf{n}_1+u\mathbf{n}_2+v\mathbf{n}_3\). The boolean intersection function uses the same calculation without building a hit record.

For a sphere, let \(\mathbf{s}=\mathbf{o}-\mathbf{c}\), where \(\mathbf{c}\) is its center and \(R\) its radius. Substitution gives \(at^2+2bt+c=0\), with \(a=\mathbf{d}\cdot\mathbf{d}\), \(b=\mathbf{s}\cdot\mathbf{d}\), and \(c=\mathbf{s}\cdot\mathbf{s}-R^2\). A negative discriminant means no hit.

To reduce cancellation, the roots are computed as \(q/a\) and \(c/q\), where \(q=-b-\mathrm{copysign}(\sqrt{b^2-ac},b)\). If \(q=0\), both roots are \(-b/a\). The nearest root within the ray interval is selected, including the exit root for rays starting inside. The normal points from the center to the hit.

The code compiled with Apple Clang. Checks passed for camera transforms, clipping, triangle boundaries and degeneracies, sphere tangencies and interior rays, and pixel sample counts. A Cornell spheres test render was also inspected.

These normal renders use 480 × 360 pixels, 64 samples per pixel, and eight threads. The teapot and banana are small meshes with about 2,500 polygons each, while the spheres test analytic intersections. A saved camera frames the whole teapot without changing its geometry.

Part 2: Bounding Volume Hierarchy

Each BVH node stores a bounding box and a range of primitives. Ranges of at most four primitives become leaves. Larger ranges split at the median along the longest axis of their centroid bounds using std::nth_element, then recurse.

The median keeps the tree balanced even when centroids coincide. The longest axis helps separate the children, but does not guarantee minimal overlap. Construction takes expected O(n log n) work. Traversal can still take O(n) when many boxes overlap.

The box test intersects the three slab intervals with the ray and caller intervals. For a zero direction component, the origin must lie inside that slab. This avoids division by zero and accepts rays inside or touching a box. A failed test leaves the caller interval unchanged.

Traversal skips missed boxes and visits the nearer child first. It then checks the other child against the updated max_t, since the first box entered may not contain the closest primitive. Shadow queries stop at any hit. This follows the acceleration lecture.

These figures use 16 samples per pixel at 480 × 360, with normal shading and adaptive sampling disabled. Beast has 64,618 source polygons and adds a detailed character mesh alongside dragon and Lucy. A saved camera keeps its head, hands, and feet inside the frame.

Each timing is the median of three runs on the local Apple Silicon Mac at 480 × 360, one sample per pixel, and one thread. Both builds use normal shading, identical compiler settings, and no adaptive sampling. The brute force build checks every primitive without box rejection. Timings exclude loading, BVH construction, and image writing. Raw logs are in assets/logs/.

Part 3: Direct Illumination

The diffuse BSDF is \(\rho/\pi\), so integrating its cosine weighted response returns the reflectance \(\rho\). Zero bounce radiance is surface emission. Direct reflection estimates

\[ L_o(\mathbf{x},\omega_o)=\int_{H^2}f_r(\omega_o,\omega_i)L_i(\mathbf{x},\omega_i)\cos\theta_i\,d\omega_i. \]

BSDF values and cosines use a local frame whose z axis is the surface normal. Rays and light directions use world space. The radiometry lecture explains the cosine term, and the Monte Carlo lecture explains division by the sampling PDF.

Uniform hemisphere sampling uses N directions, where N is the light count times the samples per light. Each ray reads emission from its first hit and contributes \(f_r L_e\cos\theta/p\), with \(p=1/(2\pi)\). The results are averaged. EPS_F prevents self intersection. Most samples miss small lights, and random rays cannot find ideal point lights.

Importance sampling asks each light for a direction, radiance, distance, and PDF. Samples below the surface or with nonpositive PDFs are rejected. Shadow rays run from EPS_F to the light distance minus EPS_F. Visible samples contribute \(f_r L_i\cos\theta/p\). Contributions are averaged per light and summed. Delta lights use one sample, while area lights use the requested count.

The comparisons use 64 camera samples, 32 samples per light, depth one, and no adaptive sampling. Hemisphere sampling leaves visible grain across the walls and objects. Light sampling produces smoother surfaces and shadows because it samples the emitter directly. These Cornell scenes have area lights and matching emissive geometry, so both estimators can sample the same source. The area light emits in one direction, which also explains differences near the ceiling emitter.

The bunny sweep uses one camera sample and 1, 4, 16, or 64 samples per area light. The speckled shadow around the feet becomes smoother as the light sample count increases. Standard error falls roughly as \(1/\sqrt{N}\), so four times as many samples halves it. Jagged silhouettes remain because the camera still takes only one sample.

Part 4: Global Illumination

Indirect lighting samples the diffuse BSDF with \(p(\omega_i)=\cos\theta_i/\pi\). The sampled direction becomes a world space ray with one less remaining bounce. Returned radiance is weighted by \(f_r\cos\theta_i/p\), which simplifies to reflectance \(\rho\) for this sampler.

Accumulation adds direct lighting at every visited surface. Without accumulation, only the last permitted surface contributes, isolating one bounce order. Depth zero shows emission, depth one direct reflection, and depth two the first indirect reflection. Camera emission appears only in accumulated renders or the isolated zeroth bounce. Recursive hits do not add emission again, since direct lighting already accounts for it.

The indirect only build omits camera emission and direct lighting at the first hit, keeping bounce two onward. In Wall-E, direct light dominates the exposed panels. The dimmer indirect image shows reflected light around the tracks, neck, and base. Combining them brightens these regions without the strong color bleeding seen in the Cornell box.

The first indirect bounce is always attempted when depth allows it. Later continuations survive with probability 0.65, and surviving contributions are divided by 0.65 to preserve their expectation. Direct lighting at the current surface is unchanged. Roulette does not remove bias from the depth cap. The isolated and accumulated bounce comparisons disable roulette.

This follows the recursive rendering equation in the global illumination lecture. The 1024 spp examples pair the Cornell bunny with the supplied Wall-E scene. The bunny shows red and blue color bleeding, while Wall-E adds detailed surfaces and narrow gaps where reflected light matters.

Each row compares bounce m alone with bounces zero through m at 1024 spp and four light samples, without roulette. The second bounce carries light into areas hidden from the source. The third reflects that light again. Wall colors transfer between surfaces, while absorption weakens later contributions. Basic rasterization with local shading lacks this exchange of light between surfaces. Accumulated images should brighten as bounces are added, though sampling noise can obscure small differences.

This sweep uses 1024 spp and four light samples at depths 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, and 100. Indirect lighting begins at depth two. Larger caps allow longer paths, but roulette ends most paths earlier. Later bounces should change the result less because each diffuse reflection reduces throughput.

The Wall-E sample sweep fixes depth five and four samples per light, with adaptive sampling disabled. More camera samples reduce noise on the body, tracks, and ground. Increasing from 1 to 1024 spp gives an expected reduction in standard error of about 32 times, but cannot fix depth truncation. The final panel reuses the Wall-E global illumination render.

Part 5: Adaptive Sampling

Adaptive sampling tracks luminance mean μ and squared deviations M₂ using Welford's updates: \(\delta=x_n-\mu_{n-1}\), \(\mu_n=\mu_{n-1}+\delta/n\), and \(M_{2,n}=M_{2,n-1}+\delta(x_n-\mu_n)\). This gives the assignment's sample variance while avoiding cancellation from subtracting large, nearly equal values.

\[ s^2=\frac{M_2}{n-1},\qquad I=1.96\sqrt{\frac{s^2}{n}},\qquad I\leq\text{maxTolerance}\,\mu. \]

The stopping condition is checked at batch boundaries after at least two samples. Sampling ends when it passes or reaches the cap. RGB values are averaged over the actual sample count, which is also saved for the rate map. A batch size of zero disables adaptive stopping. The confidence interval is approximate and can miss rare bright paths.

Bunny and Wall-E use a cap of 2048 samples, batches of 64, tolerance 0.05, one light sample, depth five, and roulette. Rate maps show samples used divided by the cap, from blue for fewer samples to red for more. The bunny needs more samples on the ceiling and shadowed surfaces. Wall-E has a blue background but red regions across its front panels, wheels, and eye rims. The maps show how sampling concentrates on the model while saving work in empty space.
